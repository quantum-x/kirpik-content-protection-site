The DMCA's reputation keeps merchants from using it. It sounds like courts and legal fees, so stolen content that could have come down within a week stays up for years instead. The reality is more ordinary: a takedown notice is a structured letter, most hosting companies process thousands of them through web forms, and the whole exercise is closer to filing a well-organised complaint than to litigation. This article walks the process for stolen store text, meaning product descriptions, blog articles and pages lifted onto someone else's site. It is general information about how the process works, not legal advice, and copyright law varies by country; for high-stakes cases, or if the other side pushes back formally, talk to a lawyer.

## Capture the evidence before anything else

Infringing pages have a habit of changing once complaints start arriving, so record everything first. Note every infringing URL, take dated screenshots, and save each page as a file. Request an Internet Archive snapshot of the infringing page as well, so a record with a date on it exists outside your control.

This is also the moment to establish that the text is provably yours rather than merely similar. If the content was protected with Kirpik, run the infringing URL through detection and generate a Proof of Ownership Certificate: it packages your protection date, the content fingerprints, the detection details and a public verification link into one dated document, which is the shape of evidence everything below wants.

## Find the company that hosts the site

A DMCA notice goes to a service provider, usually the company that hosts the thief's site. The law gives such companies a safe harbour: they are not liable for what customers upload, provided they remove infringing material when properly notified, and that incentive is why providers have reason to act on valid notices.

Identifying the host takes minutes: free public domain-record lookups usually name the company hosting the site. If the site sits behind a network such as Cloudflare, you will see the network rather than the true host, but its abuse process forwards reports onward or identifies the host to you. If the copycat runs on a hosted platform, the platform itself is your target, and things get easier: Shopify, for one, publishes its own copyright complaint form. For US providers there is a public directory of designated DMCA agents at the US Copyright Office, though searching the company's name together with "DMCA" usually lands directly on the submission page it prefers. Use that channel; it is the fastest one they process.

## Write a notice with every required element

The law lists what a valid notice must contain, and hosting companies do reject notices that miss elements, so treat this as a checklist:

1. Identification of the copyrighted work: the text that was copied, with URLs to the pages where your original lives. If you hold a Proof of Ownership Certificate, cite it here and include its public verification link.
2. The exact URLs where the copied text appears, one for every infringing page.
3. Your contact details: name, address and email.
4. A good-faith statement: that you believe the use is not authorised by the copyright owner, its agent or the law.
5. An accuracy statement: that the notice is accurate and, under penalty of perjury, that you are the owner or authorised to act for the owner. Take this line seriously; knowingly false notices carry real consequences.
6. Your signature, physical or electronic. A typed full name is widely accepted.

Keep it short and factual. Abuse teams work through queues, and the notice that states the elements cleanly, with evidence the reader can check rather than weigh, is the one handled without a clarifying email.

## What happens after you send it

Straightforward cases can resolve within days: the company removes the material or disables the page and notifies its customer. If a week or two passes in silence, follow up once through the same channel.

The person who posted the material can respond with a counter-notice claiming mistake or misidentification. If that happens the company forwards it to you, and after roughly ten to fourteen business days the material can be restored unless you inform the company that you have filed a court action. Counter-notices to clear-cut text theft are rare, because sending one means swearing to the claim and accepting a court's jurisdiction over text scraped in an afternoon, and solid evidence makes them rarer still. If one arrives, that is the moment to involve a lawyer.

The DMCA is US law, but the pattern is international: the EU, the UK and most major jurisdictions operate notice-based removal regimes, and large providers everywhere accept DMCA-style notices because the format became the industry standard. If a provider ignores you, stop waiting on that provider and use the search-removal route instead: ask the search engines to remove the infringing pages from their results, which works no matter where the server sits.

Every step above goes faster when ownership is something the reader can check rather than a story they must judge.
