Good apparel text is one of the few things on a product page a competitor cannot simply buy. The garment itself often comes off the same production line as three other labels' versions. Photography can be reshot from a sample in an afternoon. But the text that tells a shopper how a fabric drapes, whether to size up for the oversized fit, and what the piece pairs with is accumulated knowledge, built from returns data, customer emails and seasons of fittings. Which is exactly why it gets stolen.

## What fashion product text actually carries

A strong apparel description is denser than it looks. It usually contains at least four kinds of hard-won information:

- **Sizing guidance.** "Fits true to size; size up for a relaxed fit." "Model is 175 cm and wears a size S." Garment measurements versus body measurements, and the note about the high waist that saves a hundred returns a season. This is the text shoppers scroll to first and the text clones need most, because they have never held the garment.
- **The fabric story.** 230 gsm loopback cotton, brushed on the inside. Mulesing-free merino. OEKO-TEX certified dye house. A shopper cannot feel fabric through a screen, so your description does the feeling for them, and it only reads convincingly because someone who handled the cloth wrote it.
- **Styling notes.** What it layers under, which trouser it balances, whether it reads smart enough for the office. Styling text is taste in sentence form, and taste is precisely what a catalogue-cloning operation does not have on staff.
- **Care and honesty.** Cold wash, dry flat, expect slight softening after the first wash. The unglamorous lines that build trust and cut disputes.

None of this is generic. Two stores selling near-identical tees from the same factory should still have completely different descriptions, because they fit different bodies, photograph different models and serve different customers. When two stores have the same description down to the model's height, one of them copied.

## Who lifts it, and how

Apparel text travels along a few well-worn routes. Fast-fashion cloners produce a lookalike garment and take your description with it, since your sizing notes are close enough and writing fresh ones costs money. Dropshippers importing the same factory item grab whichever store's text ranks best. Marketplace sellers paste your description into their listing because the listing form demands text and yours converts.

Very little of this is done by hand. Every Shopify store exposes structured product data that scrapers read directly, no browsing required; the details are in [the public product feed you cannot turn off](/learn/shopify-public-product-feed/). A cloning toolkit can pull a full catalogue of titles, descriptions and variants in minutes, which is why copied apparel text so often keeps your exact line breaks, your "175 cm" model note and, in careless cases, your brand name mid-sentence.

## Seasonal drops compress everything

Fashion theft has a rhythm other niches do not. A drop earns most of its revenue in a short window while the trend is live. A clone that appears in week two of that window competes with you for the searches you created, and a takedown that lands after week ten protects nothing but pride.

That timing changes what protection has to look like. There is no room for a slow investigation to establish whose text this originally was. When you spot a clone mid-season, you need to move from "this reads like mine" to documented, dated proof in a day, not a month, so the takedown notice goes out while the season is still worth defending.
