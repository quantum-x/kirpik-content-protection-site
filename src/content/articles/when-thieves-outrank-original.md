The scenario below is a composite, not a story about any specific store. It is assembled from the pattern that repeats whenever merchants compare notes about copied descriptions and lost rankings. The details are typical; the names are deliberately absent. It is worth walking through slowly, because every stage contains a decision point where the outcome could have gone differently.

## The setup

Picture a small homewares store, two years old, with descriptions the owner wrote herself. One product, a linen table runner, does especially well in search. The description is genuinely good: specific fabric details, care instructions phrased in her voice, a paragraph about how the weave behaves over time. It ranks on the first page for a cluster of long-tail queries, and those queries convert.

In March, a scraper reads her catalogue through the store's [public product feed](/learn/shopify-public-product-feed/). She notices nothing, because there is nothing to notice.

## The copy appears

In April, the description surfaces on a general home-goods site, word for word, attached to a similar runner from the same overseas supplier. The copying site is older, has a few thousand referring domains from years of content marketing, and publishes at volume.

For a few weeks, both pages rank. Then the search engine's duplicate handling does what it is designed to do: it clusters the matching text and selects one version to represent it. The choice is made on signals, and the signals favour the copier.

## The signals that decided it

Search engines do not know who wrote a paragraph. When the same text lives at two URLs, canonical selection leans on proxies:

- **Domain authority.** The copier's site had years of links. Hers had dozens. On raw authority, it was not close.
- **Crawl and index history.** Her page had been indexed for a year, which helped, but recency of crawl and site-wide freshness favoured the high-volume publisher.
- **Page context.** The copier embedded the description in a longer page with related content and internal links. Her product page was, structurally, a typical product page.
- **First-indexed date.** The one signal that should have saved her, and partially did: her version was indexed first. But indexing dates are one input among many, not a trump card.

The result: for several of the long-tail queries her own sentences used to win, the copier's page now appears and hers is filtered. Traffic to the product drops over a quarter. Nothing flags this to her. The [rankings article](/learn/stolen-copy-seo-damage/) explains this machinery in general terms; here it is in miniature.

## The discovery, and the first dead end

She finds out in June, searching a phrase from her own description to check something unrelated. The copier's page comes up above hers, carrying her words.

Her first instinct is the common one: email the site and ask them to take it down. No reply. Second instinct: report it to the search engine with a form and a link to her page. The claim goes in with the evidence she has, which amounts to "my page also has this text and I say I wrote it first". Both pages exist now; nothing she has submitted establishes which came first or that she is the author. The claim stalls.

This is the moment that matters. Complaints fail on proof, not on merit.

## What evidence changes the outcome

The claims that succeed share a structure. They demonstrate three things, each independently checkable:

1. **The text existed on the claimant's site before it existed on the infringer's.** Internet Archive snapshots are the workhorse here; an earlier crawl of your page containing the disputed text is hard to argue with. We cover the technique in [using the Internet Archive to prove who published first](/learn/wayback-machine-timeline-evidence/).
2. **The match is exact and substantial.** Not "similar vibes" but identical passages, ideally fingerprinted, so the reviewer does not have to eyeball two pages.
3. **The claimant is the specific origin.** This is the hardest leg. Archive dates prove earlier publication; they do not tie the text to you as author against, say, a third store claiming the same. Evidence generated from the text itself, tied uniquely to your store, closes that gap.

With that package, the routes open up: a DMCA notice to the copier's host, and a copyright removal request to the search engine, both covered in the [enforcement guide](/enforcement/). Removal collapses the duplicate cluster back to a single version, hers, and the long-tail queries come back to the page that earned them. What counts as proof, in fuller legal-adjacent detail, is the subject of [what counts as proof when your copy is stolen](/learn/proving-text-ownership/).

## The uncomfortable moral

The original lost, for a while, because originality is invisible to an algorithm and she could not make it visible to a reviewer. Every store that publishes good copy is exposed to the same sequence, and the exposure starts the day the copy goes live, when it is easiest to scrape and hardest to have already proven.
