Most stolen descriptions never outrank the store that wrote them. The expensive minority do, and when it happens the store loses the exact queries its own sentences used to win. This article explains how that result comes about and what reverses it. It is also the outcome most worth preparing for, because the preparation is cheap and the recovery is slow. The general background, including why search engines show only one version of duplicated text, is in [what stolen content does to your search rankings](/learn/stolen-copy-seo-damage/).

## How the original loses

When the same text exists on two sites, a search engine elects one version to show. Authorship is not part of the vote, because no machine-readable signal proves who wrote a paragraph. The vote runs on what can be measured: the strength of each domain, the links pointing at each page, how often each site is crawled, and how each page performs once shown.

Every one of those measures can favour a copier. A marketplace listing sits on one of the strongest domains on the web. An established competitor has years of links a young store lacks. A frequently crawled site gets its version indexed within hours while a small store waits days for a recrawl. None of this requires bad luck, only a thief whose platform is bigger than yours, which is the usual case.

Indexing order matters more than merchants expect. If the copier's version is crawled today and your page has not been revisited since last month, the search engine meets the text on the copier's site first, and meeting the text there first is one of the few tie-breakers it has. Large sites are crawled constantly. Small stores wait.

## A composite example

What follows is a composite, not an account of any real store. A merchant sells leather goods and writes her own descriptions. One of them, for a belt, ranks well for a cluster of specific queries and brings steady orders. A marketplace seller sourcing a similar belt copies the description into his listing. The marketplace domain outranks hers within weeks, her page is filtered as the duplicate, and the orders that came through those queries move with the ranking. The displacement takes about six weeks, and at no point does anything look broken: her store loads, her page stays indexed, and the traffic is simply elsewhere.

Her first response makes it worse. She rewrites the description, reasoning that new text will end the duplication. It does, in the least helpful way: the queries that matched her old sentences now match only the marketplace listing, which holds the last remaining version of the text that ranked. The thief keeps the traffic, and she starts from nothing with unproven text. Rewriting the original is the one move that hands the copier an uncontested win.

## Getting the ranking back

The way back is removal. When the infringing version is taken down, the duplication ends and the filtering ends with it. Three routes run in parallel: a report to the marketplace or to the company hosting the thief's site, a request to search engines to remove the infringing page from results, and, where the seller is on a commerce platform, a report to the platform. [Our enforcement guide](/enforcement/) sets out each one.

All three routes decide the same question: who published this text first. The answer needs dates that a stranger can verify. An archived snapshot of your page helps, and [the Internet Archive can corroborate a timeline](/learn/wayback-machine-timeline-evidence/). The strongest position is a dated record of the exact text made before the theft, because it removes the argument entirely.

Send the report to the marketplace first, since it controls the listing directly, and file the search removal request in parallel rather than afterwards; the two do not wait for each other. Once the listing is gone and your page has been recrawled, the filtering that hid it ends, and the original competes on its own signals again.
